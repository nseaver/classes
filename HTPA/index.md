## Overview

Here's the idea: It seems obvious, maybe especially obvious these days, that attention is important. We worry about our ability to pay attention, we worry about other people's ability to pay attention, and companies make a lot of money by attracting, and maybe abusing, their users' attention. Some people argue that many of our Big Problems—from climate change to political extremism—can be explained by the shape of human attention. Attention seems intuitive, universal, and good, but also fragile, elusive, and under threat.

The goal of this class is to undermine that idea. How has attention come to seem like an all-purpose explanation for why things happen? What is attention anyway? How might our ideas about attention's goodness work against us? We'll critically examine attention by considering it in particular social, cultural, and technical contexts: we pay attention, value attention, and measure attention in a world full of other people and goals. Attention—the concept, the symbol, the mental phenomenon itself—is always tangled up in this world. That tangle is what we'll study in this class.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the semester, you should be able to:

1.  Read theoretical texts critically and generously, locating them within broader social and cultural contexts.
2.  Use anthropological concepts to analyze situations and objects from outside of the course.
3.  Think expansively and critically about the meaning and value of "attention" in public discourse.

## Assignments

A typical week in the course looks like this: I will give you a packet that contains the readings and an exercise for the week, a couple weeks before you need it. You’ll need to bring at least the week’s readings and your commonplace book to class. On top of that weekly cadence, there are a variety of other assignments, listed below.

The work in this course is designed to help us collaboratively explore ideas and to practice new ways of thinking about attention. Because the point is to practice, not to show off, I try to avoid assigning grades to as much of your work as I can, instead giving points for serious engagement with the work. Everything except the two papers is graded in this way, effectively pass/fail according to whether you’ve met the specs laid out in the assignment description, with no partial credit. If you have any questions about the specifications for a given assignment (I try to be as clear as I can, but there are always possible points of confusion), let me know ASAP. ### Reading

Each week of the class, you’ll need to read several article- or chapter-length texts, loosely connected around a given topic. In some cases, the connections won’t be obvious; thinking about connections among the readings is your job.

We can’t possibly talk about everything in all the readings in class; our discussion will follow the interests of the people who participate, so you should participate! Parts of the readings we don’t get to may nevertheless be important for arguments you want to make in your papers, and you’re always welcome to sign up for office hours if you want to talk more about topics we didn’t manage to fit into class time.

I expect that you will be actively annotating and making the most of the physicality of these readings, and there will be times set aside during class for sharing and talking about your annotations with each other. If you lose a packet or miss class, you can find the reading list on the course website, and in most cases you should be able to locate the texts online.

Lastly, these readings can sometimes be hard to understand. I expect you to do your best to make sense of them, to look up terms or supporting literature as needed to figure out what’s going on, and to spend enough time reading to get something out of it. Keep in mind that, according to Tufts’ credit hour policy, you should expect to spend 6 hours a week on this course outside of the classroom. Students who come to me with worries about understanding the reading are almost always not spending long enough on it.

### Exercises

Each week also includes some kind of exercise, which is an activity for you to do out in the world, related to the week’s theme, to document in your commonplace book. You should treat these exercises like readings: they provide a different kind of material for us to discuss and draw connections with in class. Some of them will make more sense if you have done the week’s reading first. I will be checking that these have been completed during class. 

### Commonplace book (22 points, weekly from 9/15)

A commonplace book is a traditional journal-like book for keeping quotations, ideas, and other bits of text or paper that you want to collect in one place.

I’ll be providing you with a plain composition book on the first day of class to use as your commonplace book for the term. If you’re a notebook enthusiast and would prefer to provide your own, you’re welcome to. The only requirement is that it be completely dedicated to this class, as you’ll be asked to turn it in to me occasionally.

#### Specifications

Each week, your notebook should contain, at minimum:
- One passage from each of the week’s assigned readings that you found interesting, with a page reference.
- A satisfactory question or observation of your own about each passage, for us to discuss in class.
- Documentation of each week’s exercise, in whatever form the prompt asks for.

By *satisfactory,* I mean that your questions and observations should go beyond basic matters of fact (“What does the author mean?”) and aim for broader, more interesting goals: Why does their argument matter? What is unexpected about it? How does it fit with or diverge from other readings from the week or earlier in the semester? Questions that could have been asked without doing the reading are generally not satisfactory.

These notes are also meant to support you in class. If you are not comfortable speaking off the cuff, you are welcome to read out a passage and question you’ve already written.

The quotes, questions, and exercise documentation are just the minimum. I encourage you to be expansive and creative with your commonplace book—enjoy the affordances of paper and feel free to decorate, tape things in, and so on.

You should also use the notebook for taking notes on class discussions, to gather your thinking toward your papers, and anything else related to the class. Be creative!

Please date the pages. You may want to number them as you go and leave the first couple pages blank so you can keep an updating table of contents. 

#### Assessment

I’ll check your notebooks weekly during class, stamping pages that meet the specs. You’ll get one point for the reading notes and one for the exercise. I’m looking to see that the work is there and that it meets the minimum specifications. It does not have to be clever or smart; if your work is riding the line of being unsatisfactory, I’ll tell you and expect that future entries will be up to spec.

Note that if you are absent, then I can’t stamp your book, and you can’t get credit for the work. This is intentional: your preparation for class only matters when you come to class to share it with us. Once during the semester, I’ll accept these assignments late, in the following class period. (This is a de facto attendance policy; see more on that below.)

### Portfolio (19 points, due Friday 12/18)

At the end of the semester, you’ll build a portfolio out of your commonplace book. The assignment is to read back through your notebook and make five selections from it to annotate and share. A “selection” is an exercise or one week’s reading notes. Annotations should be a clearly distinct layer of markings on top of the original (for instance, you may have copied out passages in pencil and you could return to them with colored pen, or you could layer in post-its on top of your old work) that expand on the original material, linking it to ideas from other weeks of the class, identifying something you missed the first time around, and generally drawing out themes that have come to interest you over the course of the semester.

You’ll also submit a one-page cover letter with your portfolio that explains why you chose your selections and what you think is interesting or significant about them as a group. (You might, for instance, pick pages that all relate to the same theme, or which illustrate a change in your thinking over the semester.)

Each selection counts for 3 points, plus 4 for the cover letter. I’ll be sharing more detailed specifications toward the end of the semester, but you should be anticipating this annotation layer as we move through the course.

### AI Usage

The point of college is to use your brain and to learn new ways to use your brain. ChatGPT is not your brain. Using AI to summarize readings, write papers, or answer discussion questions is like bringing a forklift to the gym: What is the point of being here?

I discourage the use of LLMs for any aspect of this course. These technologies are still new, and it is possible that there are ways to use them that don’t short-circuit your learning, but they are not designed for that, and by default, they *will* interfere with your ability to learn. If you think you have a use that’s an exception, talk to me about it.

The one hard-line AI rule in this class: **Do not submit computer-generated text as though it were your own writing.** I’m not here to evaluate the output of machines, the writing fellows are not here to teach computers how to write, and you are not here to learn how to prompt AI. You will be wasting everyone’s time. If you submit work that is obviously AI-generated (e.g., it includes hallucinated citations or quotes, or framing text like "as a large language model..." or "sure, I can write that for you..."), I will return it to you ungraded and you will receive a 0 on that assignment. If you submit work that *seems* like it is AI-generated (e.g., it is formulaic, does not seriously engage course materials, or reaches for obvious, perfunctory conclusions), then I will return it to you with the expectation that you will revise it to make it better (which is what I would do if you had generated such a submission with your own brain, too). I do not use AI-detection tools on your work (aside from my brain, which is very fine-tuned to the signatures of LLM-generated prose).

### Academic Integrity

Like all your courses, this one is predicated on the idea that it's you who's doing the work. If you don't do your own work, you don't learn, and there's little point in you being enrolled in the class. Academic integrity issues usually arise when students feel overwhelmed by work and panic. If you start to feel overwhelmed, talk to me early so we can find better ways to manage the situation.

I encourage you to take an expansive view of what academic integrity means, grounded in a practical understanding of the point of being in college (to learn). Humans communicate with words and concepts, and in genres and situations, that are never entirely original. Communication always involves combining existing materials in meaningful ways. Different groups of people have different norms that govern these combinations: DJs, poets, programmers, lawyers, and physicists all abide by different sets of rules about what counts as "original," what kinds of copying are okay (and when), and how you should relate to the materials that you draw from. Sometimes, these rules change. In this class, we'll practice the norms of citation and attribution currently shared by social scientists (and, more generally, by colleges in the US).

Rather than expending your energy worrying about originality, I suggest that you think instead about what kind of citational network you are locating yourself in and how your emerging thinking relates to the sources you're working from. What thinkers are you thinking with? Where do they come from? How might their positions in the world inform their thoughts? What is your position relative to them? How might you re-shape your citational network to better reflect your priorities or ideals?

Failure to abide by these norms is considered plagiarism, as laid out in the [Tufts Academic Integrity Policy](https://students.tufts.edu/community-standards/academic-integrity/academic-integrity-overview), which you should be familiar with. If I suspect that you've violated this policy, I am required by the university to report you to the Dean of Students, at which point the consequences are out of my hands. I do not spend much effort on detective work, but I do not recommend testing those limits. I also do not use plagiarism detection services like Turnitin (which make money by sucking it out of academic institutions, appropriating your work, and cultivating hostile relationships between students and instructors).
